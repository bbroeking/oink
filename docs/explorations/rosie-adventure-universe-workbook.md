# Rosie's Adventure Universe - Founder Workbook

**Status:** Beyond the Hedge selected; alternatives retained as exploration history

**Created:** 2026-08-04

**Purpose:** Build a coherent world, reward system, and expandable content grammar for an idle exploration game starring Rosie.

## The goal

Design the complete creative and gameplay universe for Rosie's idle exploration loop in Tickle the Pig: define the adventure fantasy, world structure, destinations, Rosie's Bag and equipment model, encounter logic, unique discovery and reward taxonomy, collection and progression loops, return ceremonies, social/Sounder opportunities, content-production rules, and monetization boundaries. Produce structured prompts and decision exercises that let the founder explore multiple coherent directions before selecting and documenting a canonical design.

## The design sentence

> Choose where Rosie will wander, pack the two things that might matter, let time carry her through an unseen story, and welcome her home with something you could not have found any other way.

## Ratified Beyond the Hedge goal

> Design and validate **Beyond the Hedge** as Rosie's idle-adventure universe: transform the ordinary countryside immediately surrounding the Barn into a deep, cozy micro-wilderness that feels wondrous at pig height. Players should send Rosie exploring by choosing where she goes, how long she wanders, what Tool and Pack she carries, and what she intends to notice. Those preparations must create understandable differences in the encounters she has, the stories she tells, and the named discoveries she can bring Home. Every return should foreground one memorable thing, reveal how the player's care mattered, and leave a visible trace in the Barn, Bag, Field Guide, map, relationships, or Sounder. Prove the idea with a playable Clover Verge chapter that makes players eager to change one preparation choice and send Rosie out again—not to optimize a payout, but to discover what else an apparently familiar place contains.

This is an **outcome goal**, not a feature list. It commits the design to a player belief, a causal loop, an emotional return, and observable proof while leaving implementation details open.

### The player belief

> Whenever Rosie leaves the Barn, players should believe that familiar places contain impossible depth—and that Rosie may notice something nobody else would.

### What the goal commits us to

- The world begins immediately outside Rosie's existing Home.
- Pig height and Rosie's personality transform ordinary countryside into adventure.
- Preparation changes possibilities rather than percentages.
- Time changes the shape of the story rather than the quality of the payout.
- Rosie always returns safely with a story, clue, discovery, relationship beat, or useful Near-Discovery.
- Named discoveries—not generic currency—provide the emotional reward.
- Travel permanently changes something the player can revisit or display.
- The player can understand why a different choice might create a different outcome.
- The content model remains sustainable for a solo developer.

### What the goal does not commit us to

- Combat, power scores, rarity ladders, or optimized farming.
- A continent-scale fantasy world.
- A single mystery that explains every destination.
- Exact trip timers, item counts, UI layouts, or backend architecture.
- Shipping every proposed Tool, Pack, destination, or reward family.
- Monetizing impatience, discovery odds, Bag pressure, or social standing.

### Prototype proof

The Clover Verge slice succeeds when a player can:

1. Describe the place as more than “the first timer location.”
2. Explain how Rosie's Tool, Pack, or intention changed what happened.
3. Remember the headline Find by name after the return.
4. Treat a Near-Discovery as a useful promise rather than a withheld prize.
5. Point to a lasting Home, Bag, Field Guide, or map change.
6. Choose a different preparation because they formed a world-based hypothesis.
7. Say what they hope Rosie finds next.
8. Want to revisit Clover Verge even after its first Wonder is discovered.

### The kill question

> If players only ask “How long until I get better loot?”, what must change before we add another destination?

The answer should be the fantasy, causality, return, or reward—not a larger content catalog.

## What this game is

This is an **idle exploration and collection game**, not primarily an idle battler, factory manager, or timer dispatch screen.

The player's authorship comes from preparation:

1. Choose a destination.
2. Equip one Tool and one Pack.
3. Give Rosie an intention.
4. Let the adventure resolve while away.
5. Read what happened.
6. Discover what her preparation made possible.
7. Use the discovery to change the world, the Bag, or the next adventure.

## Non-negotiable design pillars

### 1. Rosie is an adventurer, not a unit

Rosie remains a character with curiosity, preferences, jokes, and stories. The player does not deploy an anonymous stat bundle.

### 2. Preparation changes possibilities

Equipment should open encounter branches, protect particular finds, reveal hidden places, or change the shape of the return. Avoid equipment whose only identity is `+10% loot`.

### 3. Absence is kind

Rosie never returns injured, ashamed, or worse because the player stayed away. An underprepared trip produces a clue, near-discovery, funny detour, or partial success.

### 4. Discoveries matter because of what they are

The headline reward should be a named object, creature, clue, story, recipe, or proof of travel. Generic currency may support the loop, but it cannot be the emotional reward.

### 5. Travel changes Home

The Barn, Bag, Field Guide, map, Rosie, or Sounder should visibly remember where the player has been.

### 6. One return, one memorable thing

Every return may include supporting materials, but it should foreground one find or event the player can remember later.

### 7. The universe must be producible by one developer

Depth comes from recombining destinations, weather, intentions, equipment affordances, encounter families, and reward families. It cannot require bespoke cinematic content for every trip.

# Part I - Choose the universe

These are intentionally different foundations. They should not be blended until one has been evaluated on its own terms.

## Direction A - Beyond the Hedge

**Fantasy:** The ordinary countryside beyond Rosie's Barn becomes enormous, mysterious, and faintly magical when seen at pig height.

**World character:** Cozy micro-wilderness. Culverts become caves, garden walls become ancient borders, abandoned picnic tables become ruins, and a storm drain becomes a forbidden underground river.

**Destinations:**

- Clover Verge
- Moonlit Creek
- The Old Orchard
- Bramble Gate
- Picnic Ruins
- Rainpipe Grotto
- Windmill Hill
- The Far Side of the Pond

**Finds:** Lost buttons, beetle shells, tiny keys, seed packets, strange feathers, bottle-cap medallions, frog eggs, weathered notes, picnic relics, and creatures with local legends.

**Strengths:**

- Naturally cozy and close to the Barn.
- Everyday objects become memorable without needing high fantasy lore.
- Easy to add destinations gradually.
- Fits Field Guide discovery beautifully.

**Risk:** It may feel too small unless the presentation makes the landscape wondrous.

**Core question:** Can the world immediately outside the Barn feel as rich as a continent?

## Direction B - The Truffle Roads

**Fantasy:** Old pig roads connect a storybook world of settlements, wild regions, festivals, and forgotten landmarks. Rosie joins a long tradition of wandering pigs.

**World character:** A broad, warm adventure atlas. Each region has its own culture, food, weather, materials, stories, and Bag traditions.

**Destinations:**

- Buttercup Borough
- The Saltgrass Coast
- Acorn Railway
- Lanternwood
- Cloudberry Moor
- The Rootcellar Kingdom
- Goose Toll Country
- The Northern Troughs

**Finds:** Regional crafts, recipes, trail badges, local curios, instruments, maps, folk costumes, creatures, letters, and pieces of lost road history.

**Strengths:**

- Supports a long-running chapter map and strong regional identities.
- Makes Bag patches, postcards, recipes, and festivals feel native.
- Provides natural seasonal expansion.

**Risk:** Content scope can balloon into a conventional fantasy RPG world.

**Core question:** Is Rosie discovering a wide world, or is the game strongest when she discovers hidden depth near Home?

## Direction C - The Lost Pigways

**Fantasy:** Beneath familiar country paths lies an ancient network built by wandering pigs: hidden doors, root tunnels, abandoned waystations, coded trail markers, and machines nobody remembers how to use.

**World character:** Cozy mystery with archaeological progression. Every trip uncovers a piece of a larger question: who built the Pigways, why were they sealed, and what were they carrying?

**Destinations:**

- The First Marker
- Rootline Station
- The Sealed Pantry
- Echo Tunnel
- Moss Clock
- The Hollow Mile
- The Cartographers' Burrow
- The Door Under the Pond

**Finds:** Map fragments, stamped tokens, broken mechanisms, trail journals, coded spoons, old uniforms, preserved provisions, and dormant waystation companions.

**Strengths:**

- Gives every collection a narrative purpose.
- Clues naturally unlock routes and equipment recipes.
- Supports strong chapter finales without combat.

**Risk:** A central mystery can make ordinary cozy finds feel irrelevant or create pressure for constant plot advancement.

**Core question:** Should every adventure contribute to one mystery, or should wandering remain valuable without a grand answer?

# Part I-B - Play the first chapter on paper

Each direction below uses the same product scope: one Home send-off, one destination, two starter Kit items, three short trips, one headline Discovery, one Near-Discovery, one visible Barn change, and one reason to return. The point is to compare experiences rather than lore summaries.

## Direction A first chapter - The Path Past Clover Verge

### Opening premise

Rosie notices that a blue button has appeared beside the Barn path after every rain, even though nobody at Home owns a blue coat. She patches a small canvas Bag, packs what fits, and decides to follow the button trail beyond the hedge.

### First destination

**Clover Verge** is a strip of apparently ordinary field made enormous at pig height: clover tunnels, a humming fencepost, a puddle that holds the whole sky, and the underside of the hedge where lost things collect.

### Starter choices

- **Tool - Wooden Spoon:** tap hollow objects, pry gently, dig shallowly, and improvise.
- **Pack - Wicker Basket:** carry one extra sturdy Find, but not wet or fragile discoveries.
- **First Intention:** “Look for something strange.”

### The first three trips

1. **The Blue Button:** Rosie follows the obvious path and returns with the button, a Clover Beetle Field Guide entry, and a report that something rang beneath the fencepost. The Spoon lets her hear the hollow place but cannot open it.
2. **The Rain-Glass Puddle:** rain changes the route. Rosie finds a translucent shard that reflects a dry sky, but the Basket cannot keep it from dissolving on the way home. This becomes the first Near-Discovery: **a piece of Creek Glass needs something waterproof**.
3. **The Hollow Fencepost:** the Spoon releases a tiny brass bell stamped with a hedgehog crest. It is not valuable because it is rare; it is exciting because ringing it causes a hidden path through the clover to fold open.

### First headline Discovery

**The Hedge Bell** becomes a Barn souvenir and permanently reveals **Hedge-Tunnel Crossing**, the route toward Moonlit Creek. Its Field Guide record includes how it was found and the Kit Rosie used.

### First equipment truth

The Wooden Spoon did not improve a percentage. It created the verbs _tap_ and _pry_, which exposed the bell. The Wicker Basket brought home an extra button but failed honestly at preserving Creek Glass. The player now wants a Dry Bag for a named reason.

### First Home change

The Hedge Bell hangs beside Rosie's Bag. When tapped, it plays its small note and briefly shows the clover path opening on the map. The blue button becomes the first patch sewn onto the Bag.

### First Sounder hook

Every player finds a different stamped Hedge Bell. A Sounder can compare the animal crests and collectively reveal a route illustration, but nobody loses personal progress by playing alone.

### Thirty-day promise

The first month expands a ring around Home: Clover Verge, Moonlit Creek, the Old Orchard, and Rainpipe Grotto. Familiar destinations deepen through weather, time, and Kit combinations. The chapter ends when discoveries from all four places reveal that the countryside has its own quiet network of creature paths—not an empire or apocalypse, simply a living world Rosie has learned to notice.

### What the player should say

> “I thought I knew what was behind the Barn, but Rosie keeps finding impossible little things there.”

### Production character

Low lore burden, high prop and creature reuse, and strong compatibility with the existing Barn and Field Guide. Its principal art challenge is making small places feel wondrous rather than visually trivial.

## Direction B first chapter - Rosie's First Truffle Road

### Opening premise

A traveling post-pig arrives at the Barn with a road ribbon addressed simply to “the curious pig who lives here.” The ribbon marks Rosie as a novice wanderer and points toward Buttercup Borough, where the old Truffle Roads begin.

### First destination

**Buttercup Borough** is a small roadside settlement built around a crooked signpost, a public picnic table, a repair stall, and a departure board covered in ribbons from distant regions.

### Starter choices

- **Tool - Road Bell:** greet travelers, signal at crossings, and answer regional bell codes.
- **Pack - Patchwork Pannier:** protect one crafted or edible Find from the road.
- **First Intention:** “Meet someone new.”

### The first three trips

1. **The Unanswered Bell:** Rosie learns the greeting rhythm from a snail courier and returns with her first road stamp. A distant bell answers with an unfamiliar final note.
2. **The Buttercup Fair:** the Pannier preserves a fragile sugar-leaf rosette made by a local baker. Rosie spots a locked map case at the repair stall; it requires proof from three road traditions.
3. **The Mile-Zero Picnic:** the Road Bell completes the unanswered rhythm and calls an elderly route keeper. They gift Rosie **Mile-Zero Ribbon**, the surviving marker of the original Truffle Road.

### First headline Discovery

**Mile-Zero Ribbon** unfurls the regional atlas and reveals three possible chapter routes: the Acorn Railway, Goose Toll Country, and Lanternwood. Choosing one changes the next month of travel but does not permanently close the others.

### First equipment truth

The Road Bell creates social access; the Pannier protects cultural objects and food. Together they produce stories about exchange and hospitality rather than extraction.

### First Home change

A small departure board appears near the Bag, holding postcards, road ribbons, and future festival notices. Rosie wears the Mile-Zero Ribbon on departure days.

### First Sounder hook

Sounder members can choose different regional roads and send one another postcards that add rumors—not rewards—to unexplored destinations. A weekly crew parcel assembles a shared roadside picnic display.

### Thirty-day promise

The first month introduces Buttercup Borough and one selected region, culminating in a local festival. Progress comes from learning customs, building relationships, and bringing regional crafts Home. The horizon remains visibly larger than the released content.

### What the player should say

> “Rosie has a world of places to visit, and each place feels like it has its own people and traditions.”

### Production character

High expansion headroom and natural live-event structure, but every region asks for distinct characters, props, language, foods, and visual identity. Without strong restraint, content becomes a conventional map of themed zones.

## Direction C first chapter - The Marker Beneath the Trough

### Opening premise

While nudging a loose floorboard near the trough, Rosie finds an old spoon engraved with three arrows. At sunset, the arrows align with scratches on the Barn foundation and point toward a stone marker hidden under moss.

### First destination

**The First Marker** is a buried Pigway entrance near Home: a root-choked stair, a sealed message tube, a wall of faded route symbols, and a small mechanism that still ticks when Rosie approaches.

### Starter choices

- **Tool - Chalk Stub:** copy symbols, mark safe turns, and reveal air moving through cracks.
- **Pack - Padded Satchel:** protect one brittle or mechanical Find.
- **First Intention:** “Follow the old markers.”

### The first three trips

1. **Three Arrows:** Rosie copies the route symbol and returns with a stamped transit token. Chalk dust exposes a concealed seam, but the stone panel will not move.
2. **The Message Tube:** the Satchel preserves a fragile page naming **Rootline Station**. Half the route is written in a symbol Rosie has not learned, creating the Near-Discovery **The Missing Turn**.
3. **The Ticking Wall:** Rosie notices the spoon's three arrows match the mechanism. Using it as an improvised key opens the marker and reveals a tiny brass route model that lights one buried line at a time.

### First headline Discovery

**The First Pigway Map** becomes an interactive Barn artifact. It reveals Rootline Station and one unnamed branch, while leaving the central question intact: why was a functioning travel network deliberately hidden?

### First equipment truth

Chalk creates knowledge and safe navigation. The Padded Satchel saves evidence that would otherwise crumble. The humble spoon matters because of its fictional history, not because it is a high-rarity Tool.

### First Home change

A map table appears beneath the Bag. Each Clue physically fills a line, symbol, or station on the brass model. The copied three-arrow mark becomes Rosie's first Bag patch.

### First Sounder hook

Each member may uncover a different rubbing from the same weekly Pigway wall. Combining categories of symbols reveals a communal route story; contribution quantity is irrelevant.

### Thirty-day promise

The first month opens The First Marker, Rootline Station, and The Sealed Pantry. It culminates in restoring one harmless Pigway function: a message tube that can carry tiny gifts between Barns. Each answer reveals a more specific question rather than endlessly withholding the premise.

### What the player should say

> “Every ordinary object might connect to the Pigways, and I need to know what happened here.”

### Production character

Strong narrative momentum and economical reuse of symbols, mechanisms, and clues. It requires careful mystery planning and enough non-plot discoveries that returning to a solved destination still feels worthwhile.

## Selection result

**Beyond the Hedge** is the canonical creative direction and the first prototype foundation.

It makes the smallest promise and creates the largest amount of usable discovery. It begins at the existing Barn, lets ordinary Tickle the Pig objects become magical through Rosie's scale and attention, and can borrow a light layer of Pigway-like mystery later without making every Find serve a plot. Its content can expand outward or deepen inward without requiring a continent of cultures at launch.

The recommendation does **not** mean blending all three directions:

- Keep the **world foundation** of Beyond the Hedge.
- Borrow the **tradition and travel marks** of Truffle Roads only if the world later needs regional culture.
- Borrow the **buried-route mystery** of Lost Pigways only as one destination chain, not as the explanation for the whole universe.

## The first decision prompt

Read only the three “What the player should say” lines, then complete this sentence without naming a feature:

> I want players to believe __________________________________________ whenever Rosie leaves the Barn.

Choose the first chapter whose sentence most naturally proves that belief. If none does, reject all three and describe the missing emotional promise before discussing mechanics.

# Part II - Provisional adventure language

The terms explicitly used by the ratified goal are now recorded in `CONTEXT.md`. Detailed slot counts, item examples, reward families, and tuning remain prototype hypotheses.

## Adventurer Rosie

The identity Rosie takes on when leaving Home with a chosen destination and prepared equipment. This is not a separate character class or power mode. It is Rosie expressing curiosity through travel.

## The Bag

The physical and emotional symbol of the feature. The Bag visibly gains patches, tags, stains, charms, pockets, and souvenirs as Rosie travels.

The Bag should not become an unbounded inventory grid. It has two separate jobs:

1. **Preparation:** what Rosie takes out.
2. **Haul:** what Rosie can safely bring home.

Those jobs need separate language in the interface even if one illustrated Bag represents both.

## Kit

The reusable equipment selected before departure.

Recommended launch structure:

- **Tool slot:** changes what Rosie can do or perceive.
- **Pack slot:** changes what Rosie can carry, protect, or preserve.

Possible later structure:

- **Provision:** one consumable that changes Rosie's intention or encounter rhythm.

Two launch slots create meaningful combinations without turning the send-off into a loadout spreadsheet.

## Tool

A reusable object that creates a verb or opens an encounter branch.

Examples:

- Lantern: see in darkness.
- Shovel: uncover buried seams.
- Net: catch without harming.
- Chalk: mark a route and return farther.
- Bell: communicate with shy creatures.
- Magnifying Glass: identify tiny clues.
- Wooden Spoon: stir, pry, tap, and improvise.

## Pack

A reusable carrying object that changes the haul.

Examples:

- Wicker Basket: one additional ordinary find.
- Padded Satchel: carry fragile curios.
- Specimen Case: bring home living or delicate Field Guide finds.
- Dry Bag: preserve objects found near water.
- Picnic Roll: trade a find slot for longer travel.
- Sounder Sack: convert one haul slot into a pooled crew parcel.

## Intention

A single plain-language instruction that biases Rosie's behavior without guaranteeing a result.

Examples:

- Look for something strange.
- Follow the old markers.
- Bring something for the Barn.
- Help a creature.
- Find a gift for the Sounder.
- Take the safe way home.

## Find

Anything Rosie brings home. A Find is the broad result category, not necessarily a permanent collectible.

## Discovery

The first time a player encounters a distinct creature, object, place, recipe, clue, or phenomenon. Discovery is a ceremony and permanently changes an appropriate journal, map, or collection.

## Near-Discovery

Something Rosie encountered but could not fully access, identify, or carry with the current Kit. It provides a concrete clue for a future trip and prevents an underprepared adventure from feeling empty.

# Part III - The reward universe

## Rewards should perform jobs

Every reward family should answer at least one of these questions:

- What new place can I reach?
- What new encounter can happen?
- What can Rosie now carry home?
- What changed at the Barn?
- What story became clearer?
- What collection did I advance?
- What can I show another player?
- What did my Sounder accomplish together?

If a reward answers none of them, it is probably economy filler.

## Proposed reward families

### 1. Wonders

One-time headline discoveries that define a destination or chapter.

Examples: The Door Under the Pond, a living Moonflower, the original Goose Toll ledger, the bell from a vanished waystation.

**Job:** Unlock routes, stories, ceremonies, or permanent world changes.

### 2. Curios

Named collectible objects grouped into small thematic sets.

Examples: Picnic Relics, Things That Should Not Be Tiny, Lost Weather Tools, Goose Bureaucracy.

**Job:** Collection pleasure, display, completion rewards, humor.

### 3. Field Guide discoveries

Creatures, plants, weather, landscape phenomena, and adventure-economy objects.

**Job:** Make knowledge and recognition part of progression.

### 4. Clues

Map fragments, trail marks, overheard directions, coded notes, and partial recipes.

**Job:** Reveal destinations and provide long-horizon anticipation.

### 5. Patterns

Instructions for making a new Tool, Pack, Bag pocket, or upgrade.

**Job:** Convert discovery into future possibility without dropping finished gear constantly.

### 6. Materials

A deliberately small set of useful travel materials.

Examples: Waxed Thread, Soft Moss, Bell Metal, Creek Glass, Rootwood.

**Job:** Craft or improve specific adventure equipment.

Materials should stay recognizable and capped in variety. Do not create one currency per region.

### 7. Bag marks

Patches, pins, tags, stitching, mud stains, charms, and route ribbons visibly added to Rosie's Bag.

**Job:** Turn travel history into expression and status without adding power.

### 8. Barn souvenirs

Objects that visibly appear in the Interior or on an adventure shelf.

**Job:** Make travel change Home.

### 9. Sounder parcels

Finds contributed to a pooled crew objective rather than ranked by member value.

Examples: restore a bridge, fill a communal map, provision a waystation, assemble a giant regional curiosity.

**Job:** Create shared appointments and collective proof without shame rankings.

## Return composition

A strong return should usually contain:

1. **One headline result:** Wonder, new Curio, Discovery, Pattern, or Clue.
2. **Zero to three supporting finds:** known Curios or Materials.
3. **One story beat:** a named event or funny behavior.
4. **One equipment truth:** how the selected Tool or Pack mattered.
5. **Optionally one Near-Discovery:** a reason to change the next Kit.

The player should never need to process twelve unrelated reward icons after a return.

## Reward-to-gameplay loop

```text
DISCOVER A PLACE
  -> encounter something the current Kit can affect
  -> return with one memorable Find
  -> record, display, craft, or contribute it
  -> unlock a destination, branch, or equipment possibility
  -> prepare a meaningfully different Kit
  -> revisit with a new intention
  -> reveal the place's deeper layer
```

This creates **revisitation with purpose**, not merely farming a higher rarity.

# Part IV - Encounter grammar

An adventure is assembled from a small number of reusable encounter roles.

## Encounter roles

### Landmark

A memorable place that establishes the destination's identity.

### Fork

A choice Rosie resolves using her intention, Tool, or a known clue.

### Opportunity

Something the Kit can reveal, reach, catch, communicate with, protect, or carry.

### Character

A creature or traveler with a recurring personality and relationship state.

### Complication

Weather, mud, darkness, distance, bureaucracy, a locked gate, or an inconvenient goose. Complications stall or redirect; they do not injure Rosie.

### Near-Discovery

A visible possibility that names what a future Kit could change.

### Homeward Beat

The comedy, kindness, or observation that closes the trip and makes the return sound like Rosie.

## Content template

Every destination can be authored as:

```text
Destination identity
+ 2 Landmarks
+ 2 Complications
+ 3 Tool affordances
+ 3 Pack affordances
+ 2 recurring Characters
+ 1 Wonder
+ 2 Curio sets
+ 1 Material
+ 2 Clue branches
+ 6 Homeward Beats
```

Weather, intention, first visit/revisit state, and equipment combinations remix those authored pieces without inventing a separate minigame.

# Part V - Founder prompt deck

Answer these in rounds. Do not attempt all of them at once.

## Round 1 - The fantasy

1. When a player says, "Rosie is an adventurer now," what has visibly changed about her?
2. Is the emotional fantasy closer to a child exploring behind the house, a traveler crossing a storybook continent, or an archaeologist uncovering a cozy mystery?
3. What can only Rosie notice because she is a pig?
4. What would make an adventure feel unmistakably like Tickle the Pig rather than a generic dispatch timer?
5. Should the player feel that the world is vast, or that familiar places contain impossible depth?
6. What is the first destination a player should remember by name one week later?
7. What should never exist in this world, even if it is common in mobile adventure games?

## Round 2 - Rosie and the Bag

1. Does Rosie choose to become an adventurer, inherit a Bag, find it, or receive it from someone?
2. Is the Bag charming because it begins homemade and patched, or because it is an old object with history?
3. Should the Bag visibly evolve through power upgrades, travel marks, or both?
4. What are the first Tool and Pack, and what distinct stories do they create on the first day?
5. Is a Pack a swappable equipment category, or is Rosie's Bag permanent with unlockable internal pockets?
6. What does Rosie refuse to carry?
7. What item seems useless at first but becomes beloved because of the stories it creates?
8. When should the player choose an intention: before selecting equipment or after seeing the destination conditions?

## Round 3 - The world map

1. Are destinations arranged as one expanding map, chapter books, branching roads, or a rotating set of available journeys?
2. What makes revisiting the Meadow different after ten adventures?
3. Does weather change what can be found, or only the story and duration?
4. Which places are always available, and which appear only after clues or conditions?
5. Can Rosie get intentionally lost? If so, why would the player choose that?
6. What is the world's equivalent of seeing a distant mountain and wondering when you can reach it?
7. How does the map visibly remember completed discoveries without becoming covered in checklist icons?

## Round 4 - Exciting rewards

1. Name ten objects you would genuinely be excited to see Rosie pull from her Bag.
2. Which rewards should change gameplay, which should change Home, and which should exist purely because they are delightful?
3. What is the rarest possible reward category, and why is it rare in fiction rather than merely by probability?
4. Which Find should cause a friend to say, "Where did you get that?"
5. What can be completed as a three-item set rather than a fifty-item catalog?
6. What reward creates a new verb instead of a higher number?
7. Can a duplicate tell a new story, improve an object, become a gift, or contribute to a Sounder project?
8. What is the worst possible reward screen, and which design rules prevent it?

## Round 5 - The return

1. What is the first thing the player sees when Rosie returns?
2. Does Rosie unpack the Bag herself, or does the player open it with her?
3. Should the best Find be revealed first, last, or discovered unexpectedly among ordinary things?
4. What information must be visible before any reward animation finishes?
5. How do we explain that equipment mattered without showing a combat log?
6. What makes an eight-hour return feel different from a one-hour return besides quantity?
7. What line could Rosie bring home that would make the player immediately send her out again?
8. How should a Near-Discovery feel exciting instead of withholding?

## Round 6 - Progression

1. Does progression primarily expand distance, possibility, carrying capacity, knowledge, or all four in a deliberate sequence?
2. What is the first permanent change earned through travel?
3. What can an experienced player do that a new player cannot, without simply receiving more loot?
4. Are Tools found, crafted from Patterns, gifted by characters, or upgraded from humble objects?
5. What is the equivalent of prestige, if any? Could completing an atlas open a new interpretation of familiar places rather than reset progress?
6. How many meaningful Kit combinations should exist at launch?
7. What prevents one Tool/Pack combination from becoming universally optimal?
8. How does the system remain legible without a wiki after a year of content?

## Round 7 - Sounder and social play

1. What can four pigs discover together that one pig cannot?
2. Can players contribute different categories rather than different quantities?
3. What shared object would be satisfying to assemble over a week?
4. How can a player help after returning from a short trip without feeling behind?
5. What does the Sounder celebrate collectively without ranking individuals?
6. Can members leave route notes, lend intentions, or reveal opportunities without lending power?
7. What social reward becomes visible in every member's Barn or Bag?

## Round 8 - Content sustainability

1. Can a new destination be authored from the encounter template in one focused production cycle?
2. Which content requires new art, and which can be recombined from existing pieces?
3. What is the minimum viable destination that still feels like a place?
4. Which reward families can grow indefinitely, and which must remain scarce to stay special?
5. Can an event remix a known destination through weather, visitors, or a shared objective instead of adding another mode?
6. What content can return annually without feeling recycled?
7. If no new chapter shipped for three months, would the existing world still be satisfying to revisit?

## Red-team prompts

1. When does preparation become homework?
2. When does the Bag become inventory management?
3. When do Near-Discoveries feel like artificial equipment gates?
4. When do Materials turn discoveries into crafting chores?
5. When does the postcard become text players skip?
6. When does a destination stop feeling mysterious and become a drop table?
7. Which system would be most tempting to monetize in a way that damages the fantasy?
8. What could make players resent being away for the "wrong" duration?
9. What is the smallest version of this universe that would prove the emotional loop?
10. What should be deleted if the design requires more than one paragraph to explain?

# Part VII - Provisional system blueprint

This section defines the gameplay hypothesis now being tested inside Beyond the Hedge. It is specific enough to prototype, but details not committed by the ratified goal remain provisional until observed play resolves them.

## The nested loops

The game should feel complete at five different timescales.

| Timescale       | Player action                                              | Emotional payoff                | Durable result                                             |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| One check-in    | Welcome Rosie home, inspect one story, make one new choice | Surprise and affection          | A Find, story beat, or Near-Discovery                      |
| One trip        | Choose destination, duration, Tool, Pack, and intention    | “My preparation mattered”       | New possibility or useful knowledge                        |
| One destination | Revisit with different preparations                        | Growing familiarity             | Wonder, completed small collection, deeper route           |
| One chapter     | Connect discoveries across several destinations            | Adventure and resolution        | Permanent Home or map change                               |
| Long-term play  | Build Rosie's travel history                               | Belonging and personal identity | A unique Bag, Field Guide, Home, and shared Sounder record |

The short loop must remain satisfying without knowledge of the long loop. The long loop must emerge from remembered short trips rather than abstract account level.

## The send-off decision

Every departure asks five plain-language questions:

1. **Where?** Choose one destination with visible current conditions.
2. **How long?** Choose the kind of trip, not a raw timer optimization.
3. **With what Tool?** Choose the verb Rosie can use.
4. **With what Pack?** Choose what kind of Find can survive the return.
5. **With what intention?** Choose what Rosie pays attention to.

The screen should preview possibilities without revealing a drop table. It may say “dark hollows,” “fragile things,” or “a creature has been leaving marks,” but never “Lantern: +18% legendary chance.”

## Trip shapes, not timer tiers

Recommended prototype durations:

| Trip shape      | Target duration | What changes                                            | What does not change               |
| --------------- | --------------: | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **Poke Around** |      15 minutes | One nearby encounter; ideal for learning a Tool         | Reward quality                     |
| **Good Wander** |         2 hours | Two or three encounters; ordinary destination depth     | Rosie's safety or mood             |
| **Day Out**     |         8 hours | One deeper branch, distant landmark, or condition cycle | A multiplier applied to everything |

Long trips create different narrative opportunities, not simply more rolls. A short trip can still produce a headline Discovery. The player should choose a trip shape that fits real life without feeling economically irresponsible.

Later chapters may introduce overnight expeditions only after the three basic shapes are emotionally legible. No trip should require checking the game at an exact return minute.

## Adventure resolution

An adventure can be resolved from six inputs:

```text
destination state
+ trip shape
+ Tool affordance
+ Pack affordance
+ intention bias
+ prior knowledge
= ordered encounter story and return payload
```

Resolution should be deterministic enough that preparation feels causal and variable enough that revisits remain surprising.

Recommended rule:

- One encounter is selected because of the destination and current condition.
- One encounter is strongly biased by intention.
- The Tool may transform or open an encounter.
- The Pack may preserve, enlarge, trade, or redirect the result.
- Prior knowledge may replace a repeated explanation with a deeper branch.
- A small variation layer chooses character behavior, prose, and supporting Finds.

Randomness chooses among understandable possibilities. It should not erase a valid preparation choice.

## Kind failure states

There is no failed trip. There are only different qualities of knowledge and return.

| Situation                           | Return result                                                            | Next decision created                     |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Wrong Tool                          | Rosie notices an inaccessible verb or hidden feature                     | Bring the Tool that can act on it         |
| Wrong Pack                          | Rosie identifies what could not survive the trip                         | Bring the Pack that protects it           |
| Conflicting intention               | Rosie follows one lead and records the missed fork                       | Change intention on the revisit           |
| Too-short trip                      | Rosie reaches a named stopping point                                     | Choose a longer shape when convenient     |
| Duplicate Find                      | The object gains a story, restoration use, gift use, or set contribution | Decide its meaningful destination         |
| No new headline Discovery available | Rosie deepens a relationship, map note, or known object story            | Build familiarity rather than farm rarity |

The copy must name what happened in the world. “Requirement not met” is never acceptable return language.

## Progression is expanding authorship

Progression should enlarge what the player can intentionally cause. It has five interlocking tracks, none represented as a generic power score.

### 1. Reach

Clues, paths, relationships, and chapter Wonders reveal new destinations or deeper layers of known ones.

### 2. Capability

Tools add new verbs. Packs add new preservation and carrying possibilities. Upgrades broaden an item's use or add a trade-off; they do not merely increase yield.

### 3. Knowledge

Field Guide entries, character familiarity, weather observations, and route notes make future preparation more legible.

### 4. Expression

Bag marks, travel clothing, Barn displays, map annotations, and return-book styling make one player's Rosie visibly different from another's.

### 5. Belonging

Recurring characters, destination restoration, and Sounder projects give discoveries relationships and shared consequences.

Recommended first-month sequence:

```text
notice a possibility
-> learn one useful verb
-> preserve one previously lost Find
-> reveal a nearby route
-> complete one three-object Curio set
-> change the Barn
-> contribute a discovery category to a Sounder project
-> resolve the first destination Wonder
```

## The Bag model

The Bag is one illustrated object with three clearly separated layers.

### The Kit layer - what goes out

- Exactly one Tool slot.
- Exactly one Pack slot.
- No stat budget, weight arithmetic, or inventory Tetris.
- Later, at most one optional Provision slot for a consumable intention modifier.

### The Haul layer - what comes home

The prototype gives Rosie three conceptual haul spaces:

- **Headline space:** always reserved for the trip's memorable result; cannot be monetized or expanded.
- **Pack space:** governed by the equipped Pack and the type of Find it can preserve.
- **Pocket space:** one ordinary supporting Find; may be improved through earned Bag craftsmanship.

This prevents an exciting Discovery from being discarded because the player failed an inventory puzzle.

### The history layer - what the Bag remembers

Patches, route ribbons, stains, pins, repairs, and charms record milestones. Most marks are expressive, not equipable. A player should be able to point at the Bag and recount where its details came from.

## Equipment design rules

Every Tool and Pack needs five authored properties:

1. A physical fantasy Rosie can demonstrate.
2. One primary affordance written as a verb.
3. One secondary or surprising use.
4. At least one destination where it is not the best choice.
5. At least one memorable story line explaining how it mattered.

Starter set recommendation:

| Item           | Category | Primary affordance          | Surprising use                     | Natural limitation                  |
| -------------- | -------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Wooden Spoon   | Tool     | Tap and pry                 | Communicate through rhythm         | Cannot illuminate or preserve       |
| Lantern        | Tool     | Reveal in darkness          | Attract or reassure some creatures | Causes shy discoveries to hide      |
| Chalk Stub     | Tool     | Mark and copy               | Reveal drafts through cracks       | Rain removes its marks              |
| Wicker Basket  | Pack     | Carry one extra sturdy Find | Trade or share its contents        | Cannot protect wet or fragile Finds |
| Padded Satchel | Pack     | Protect fragile Finds       | Muffle a noisy object              | Holds fewer ordinary objects        |
| Dry Bag        | Pack     | Preserve wet Finds          | Float something across water       | Cannot carry sharp or living Finds  |

Six items create nine Tool-Pack combinations at launch. The first prototype only needs one Tool and two Packs to prove that changing one slot changes the story.

## Collection loops

Collections should be small, located, and expressive. Avoid a single completion screen containing hundreds of unrelated silhouettes.

### Field Guide

Records creatures, plants, phenomena, and meaningful adventure objects. Repeated encounters add behavior notes, conditions, and Rosie's observations rather than a duplicate counter.

### Curio shelves

Each Curio set contains three to seven named objects with a visible display composition. Completing a set produces a story, Home interaction, Pattern, or route clue—not premium currency.

### Adventure map

Records landmarks and unresolved possibilities. Its visual states are:

- Rumored.
- Visited.
- Familiar.
- Understood.

“Understood” does not mean exhausted. Characters, weather, and seasonal variations can continue after completion.

### Bag history

Records earned travel marks. Players choose which marks are visible, making the Bag a curated autobiography rather than a checklist.

### Return Book

Keeps a short illustrated record of headline returns. The default Book saves first Discoveries, chapter events, favorite trips, and player-pinned stories—not every generated material receipt.

## Duplicate policy

Every repeatable Find must support at least two of these uses:

- Reveal a new observation or provenance line.
- Restore or improve the displayed original.
- Complete a Pattern with a clear cap.
- Gift it to a known character.
- Place it in a Sounder parcel.
- Trade it for a specific, fictionally related object.

There is no universal dusting currency. If an item has no meaningful repeat use, it should stop appearing after discovery.

## Destination mastery without exhaustion

A destination progresses through four authored layers:

1. **First impression:** landmark, common character, ordinary Finds.
2. **Prepared revisit:** Kit-dependent branches and first Near-Discoveries.
3. **Familiar place:** relationships, condition knowledge, Curio completion.
4. **Wonder resolution:** a permanent map or Home change and a new route.

After the Wonder, the destination becomes a familiar place rather than a depleted node. Seasonal conditions, character requests, Sounder projects, and unresolved collection stories can still create worthwhile trips.

## The return ceremony

The target is a warm 20- to 35-second ceremony that can be accelerated without losing information.

### Beat 1 - Rosie is home

Show Rosie first, not a reward chest. Her appearance, Bag, weather trace, or animation communicates the shape of the trip.

### Beat 2 - The opening line

One sentence establishes the trip's memorable event: “The fencepost rang back when I tapped it.”

### Beat 3 - Preparation mattered

Show one compact equipment moment in world language: “Your Wooden Spoon found the hollow seam.” Never display hidden arithmetic as the emotional explanation.

### Beat 4 - The headline reveal

Rosie removes or reveals exactly one memorable Find. A first Discovery receives its name, collection destination, and visible consequence.

### Beat 5 - Supporting unpack

Zero to three supporting Finds appear together. Known Materials and repeats do not interrupt the headline ceremony.

### Beat 6 - The world remembers

Show what changed: a map line, Field Guide note, Barn placement, relationship beat, Pattern progress, or Sounder contribution.

### Beat 7 - The next possibility

Offer one specific reason to leave again. This may be a new destination, a Near-Discovery with a suggested affordance, a changed condition, or a character invitation.

### Beat 8 - Three exits

- **Go again:** same destination with an editable Kit.
- **Plan another:** return to the map.
- **Stay Home:** enjoy Rosie and Home without penalty.

The return is not a modal trap. The player may skip animation, but the headline, equipment truth, and world change remain readable in a summary.

## Near-Discovery contract

A Near-Discovery is only fair when it communicates:

- What Rosie encountered.
- Why the current preparation could not fully resolve it.
- What kind of capability may change the result.
- Why returning would reveal something new.

It should usually suggest an affordance, not name a required item. “Something waterproof could preserve the Rain-Glass” invites experimentation; “Equip Dry Bag level 3” reduces the world to a lock.

No destination should show more than one unresolved Near-Discovery at a time in the return ceremony.

# Part VIII - Sounder and social blueprint

Social play should make individual stories more meaningful, not turn kind wandering into comparative labor.

## Social laws

1. No member leaderboard for quantity, speed, rarity, or trip duration.
2. No daily contribution quota and no punishment for an absent member.
3. No exclusive gameplay power available only through a large or active Sounder.
4. Contributions should differ by category or perspective, not simply magnitude.
5. A player must understand what their own Rosie contributed.
6. Shared rewards should be commemorative, expressive, or route-opening for everyone.

## Three social forms

### Route notes

Players leave short, system-authored discoveries for one another: “A bell answered near the hedge after rain.” Notes reveal possibilities without solving loadouts or transferring power.

### Sounder parcels

A player voluntarily assigns one eligible supporting Find to a weekly communal parcel. The parcel asks for categories—something soft, something that rings, something from water—not rarity totals. Every fulfilled category adds a visible object to the final scene.

### Shared restoration

Several categories of discoveries restore a bridge, waystation, picnic, map, or creature habitat. Completion opens a shared scene and awards each member a dated Bag mark or Barn souvenir. Solo play can reach functionally equivalent destinations through personal routes.

## First Sounder project - The Hedgeway Picnic

The group fills five qualitative needs over one week:

- Something to sit on.
- Something that makes a tune.
- Something sweet-smelling.
- A sign that points Home.
- A story from bad weather.

Each member may fill any one category, and a single member can finish the project if necessary. Multiple valid Finds create different final picnic illustrations, so the reward reflects the group without ranking it.

## Social reward boundary

The Sounder may accelerate shared expression and reveal communal story variants. It must not increase Rosie's personal Discovery odds, shorten trip timers, or become the optimal source of Tools and Packs.

# Part IX - Content-production rules

The universe stays sustainable by authoring relationships among reusable pieces rather than writing a bespoke quest for every timer.

## Minimum viable destination packet

A shippable destination needs:

- One unmistakable visual silhouette and ambient identity.
- Two Landmarks.
- Two current Conditions, one of which may be weather.
- Two recurring Characters or creature behaviors.
- Three Tool affordances.
- Three Pack affordances.
- Three Intentions with meaningfully different biases.
- One three-to-five-object Curio set.
- Three Field Guide subjects.
- One Material with a capped use.
- One Pattern or relationship reward.
- Two Near-Discoveries.
- One Wonder and permanent consequence.
- Eight to twelve Homeward Beats.

This packet should create at least twelve legible preparation combinations before adding prose variants.

## Content hierarchy

Content should be produced in this order:

1. **Destination truth:** what this place is about emotionally.
2. **Landmarks and characters:** what the player will remember.
3. **Affordance matrix:** how Tools, Packs, intentions, and conditions change events.
4. **Reward jobs:** what each Find changes after the return.
5. **Encounter beats:** the small stories connecting preparation to reward.
6. **Variation prose:** humor, behavior, and repeat lines.
7. **Art polish:** unique assets only where identity or ceremony depends on them.

Writing loot first creates a drop table. Writing the place first creates exploration.

## Asset tiers

| Tier     | Use                                                          | Production rule                                   |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Hero     | Rosie, Bag, destination key art, Wonders, major Home changes | Bespoke and reusable across many trips            |
| Identity | Landmarks, recurring characters, Curio set display           | One clear silhouette with limited state variants  |
| Modular  | Materials, conditions, supporting Finds, map marks           | Recolor, recombine, crop, and layer deliberately  |
| Textual  | Homeward Beats, observations, route notes                    | Must change interpretation, not merely add volume |

Wonders remain special partly because they receive hero treatment. Materials must not consume hero-art capacity.

## Reuse without repetition

A known destination can become newly interesting through:

- Weather changing an affordance.
- A familiar character having a new relationship state.
- A discovered clue recontextualizing an old landmark.
- A Sounder project temporarily asking a different question of the place.
- A seasonal visitor or harmless migration.
- A new Tool creating a verb the original content already anticipated.

Events should prefer two changed relationships in a known place over one disposable event map.

## Content budget guardrails

- No destination launches without a Wonder and post-Wonder reason to revisit.
- No reward family is added without a distinct gameplay job.
- No new Material is added if an existing material can express the same fiction.
- No equipment item is added without at least three authored affordances across existing destinations.
- No chapter depends on more than one new systemic verb.
- No trip requires bespoke animation to be understandable.
- Every hero asset should appear in Home, the map, the Field Guide, or repeated return ceremonies—not once.

## Seasonal content rule

A season changes known places and relationships. It should add one condition family, one small collection, one communal project, and one lasting keepsake. The season's core emotional story remains accessible when the timed event ends; only its dated expression may be exclusive.

# Part X - Monetization boundaries

Monetization must preserve the belief that Rosie explores because she is curious and the player prepares her with care.

## Safe things to sell

- Bag shapes, fabrics, stitching styles, and purely expressive charms.
- Adventure outfits that do not affect resolution.
- Barn display furniture and alternate Curio shelf presentations.
- Return Book covers, page treatments, and optional presentation themes.
- Cosmetic map styles and route-marker treatments.
- Clearly scoped seasonal cosmetic passes with a free story track.
- Optional bundles that support the game while stating exactly what is cosmetic.

Cosmetics may commemorate discoveries, but a purchase must never counterfeit an earned route mark, Wonder, or first-Discovery story.

## Things that must remain earned

- Tools and Packs with gameplay affordances.
- Destination access and route Clues.
- Wonders, Curios, Field Guide discoveries, and headline Finds.
- Bag marks that prove travel or shared contribution.
- Additional headline or haul capacity.
- Relationship progress and Sounder project credit.

## Forbidden monetization

- Paying to finish or skip a trip.
- Energy systems that prevent sending Rosie away.
- Loot boxes, paid rerolls, rarity boosts, or Discovery odds.
- Selling the correct Tool after showing a Near-Discovery.
- Streaks that threaten loss of progress.
- Inventory pressure manufactured to sell capacity.
- Paid protection from failed, injured, sad, or empty returns.
- Sounder competition driven by purchased contributions.
- Limited-time gameplay discoveries that disappear before a normal player can reach them.

## Ads boundary

The preferred design has no interruptive advertising. If rewarded ads are ever considered, they cannot change trip time, reward quality, haul capacity, Near-Discovery resolution, or Sounder contribution. A cosmetic preview is less harmful than a progression reward, but even that must not interrupt Rosie's return.

## Ethical offer test

Before shipping an offer, ask:

1. Does this purchase express the player's Rosie, or improve her odds?
2. Would refusing it make the current trip feel worse?
3. Does it exploit impatience created by our timer?
4. Could a player mistake purchased decoration for earned history?
5. Does it make another Sounder member more valuable?

If any answer threatens preparation, discovery, or belonging, reject the offer.

# Part XI - Recommended prototype slice

This is the smallest interactive slice that can test the emotional loop without pretending to be the full game. It uses Beyond the Hedge provisionally because that direction currently has the strongest design fit.

## Included world

- Home send-off at the Barn.
- One destination: Clover Verge.
- Two conditions: dry afternoon and after rain.
- One Tool: Wooden Spoon.
- Two Packs: Wicker Basket and Dry Bag.
- Two intentions: “Look for something strange” and “Bring something for the Barn.”
- One fixed five-minute test duration, with simulated later-trip previews.

## Included content

- Two Landmarks: Hollow Fencepost and Rain-Glass Puddle.
- One recurring character: Clover Beetle.
- One Field Guide Discovery: Clover Beetle.
- One three-object Curio set: Blue Button, Bent Thimble, Hedgehog-Crest Token.
- One Material: Creek Glass.
- One Near-Discovery: dissolving Rain-Glass.
- One Wonder: Hedge Bell.
- One permanent Barn change: the mounted bell and opened Hedge-Tunnel route.
- Six Homeward Beats.
- One Sounder-project preview, not a working social backend.

## Required playable sequence

1. Send Rosie with Wooden Spoon + Wicker Basket.
2. See a short adventure-in-progress story, then leave or complete immediately in prototype mode.
3. Welcome Rosie home and learn that the Spoon exposed the Hollow Fencepost.
4. Receive one named headline Find and supporting Find.
5. See one Barn or Field Guide change.
6. Encounter Rain-Glass that the Basket cannot preserve.
7. Change only the Pack to Dry Bag.
8. Return and preserve Creek Glass, proving that preparation changes possibility.
9. Reveal the Hedge Bell and the next route.

## Prototype questions

The prototype is successful only if observation or interview can answer these:

- Can the player explain why Rosie brought home each headline Find?
- Does changing one slot create anticipation rather than configuration anxiety?
- Does the Near-Discovery feel like a useful clue rather than a denied reward?
- Is the player more excited by the named object than by its rarity?
- Does the Barn change make the adventure feel permanent?
- Does the player want to revisit Clover Verge after its Wonder is found?
- Does Rosie feel like the actor, while the player still feels authorship?
- Does the player naturally describe a story rather than a payout?

## Kill and revise criteria

- If players ask for percentages before choosing, improve condition and affordance language.
- If they ignore Rosie's story and scan rewards, reduce supporting Finds and strengthen causality.
- If the correct Pack feels obvious and compulsory, introduce two valid but different outcomes.
- If the Near-Discovery feels punitive, let it add a Field Guide observation or supporting Find immediately.
- If the return feels like opening a chest, put Rosie and her opening line before all reward UI.
- If the Wonder ends interest in Clover Verge, author a relationship or condition that only becomes available afterward.
- If players cannot remember the Find's name five minutes later, revise the object and its consequence before adding rarity effects.

## Canonization decision after testing

Do not ask only “Was it fun?” Ask the founder and players to choose which statement the prototype proved:

1. Familiar places contain impossible depth.
2. Rosie belongs to a wider culture of wandering pigs.
3. Ordinary objects are evidence of a hidden history.
4. None of these; the prototype suggests a different emotional truth.

Only then should the selected fantasy sentence, Bag language, and stable adventure concepts be promoted into canonical project documentation.
